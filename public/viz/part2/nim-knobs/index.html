Nim knobs Course page

Teach a machine to play Nim.

A pile holds 10 matches. Two players take turns, and each turn takes 1, 2 or 3 matches. Whoever takes the last match wins.

The machine moves first against a perfect opponent. It knows no strategy. It looks its move up in a table of numbers, the knobs, and you turn them.

Play a few games, see which states are good, and turn the knobs until the machine wins.

1The knobs.

One knob for every choice: how many matches to take (rows) when the pile holds so many (columns). A higher knob makes that move likelier. Every knob starts at 0.

2The probabilities.

Each column turns its knobs into probabilities: the machine takes e to the power of each knob and divides by the column’s total. A knob raised by 1 makes its move about 2.7 times as likely, compared with the others in its column.

takematches in the pile, chance in %

3Chance of winning.

0%

Untouched table: 0%. Perfect play: 100%. The number is exact: it adds up every way the game can go, each weighted by its probability.

How is the chance computed?

The machine draws its move from the probabilities of the table. The opponent takes the amount that leaves a multiple of 4 whenever it can, and from 4 or 8, where every move loses, it takes 1, 2 or 3 at random. The page adds up the probability of every game that ends with the machine taking the last match. Nothing is simulated, so the number never jiggles.

Tap a dark tag to select the knob that made that move. The small blue and orange numbers on the knobs in step 1 count the good and the bad moves of these games.

Why are some states good?

After every move the pile holds some number of matches: that is the state. Leave 4, 8 or 0 matches and the machine wins whatever the opponent does: if the opponent takes 1, 2 or 3, the machine takes 3, 2 or 1, and the pile is back at a multiple of 4. Leave anything else and the perfect opponent does exactly that to the machine. A state is already lost when the machine has to move from 4 or 8.

In the lecture the knobs are the weights w and the table of probabilities is the policy.
